TL;DR

The FBI warned on November 16 that criminals hacked more than 1,000 WordPress websites and used Google search results to lure people into downloading ransomware. Your website could be used this way without you noticing, so it is worth a quick check this week.

  • Criminals break into websites through weak passwords and outdated plugins, then use them to trick searchers.
  • The FBI ties this scheme to more than $28 million in ransomware payments.
  • Strong unique passwords, two-step login and regular updates block most of these attacks.
  • Be careful about downloading files from unfamiliar sites, even when they show up near the top of Google.

Most business owners think of Google as the safe, trusted way to find things online. A new FBI warning is a reminder that criminals know this too. They are hacking ordinary business websites and pushing them up in search results so that people searching for everyday documents end up downloading malicious software instead.

What the FBI found

On November 16, the FBI published an alert about a criminal tool called Gootloader. Here is how the scheme works, in plain terms:

  1. The criminals break into real business websites built on WordPress, usually by using stolen passwords or weaknesses in outdated plugins and themes.
  2. They quietly add pages and hidden links to those sites so the pages show up in Google for very specific searches, such as a sample contract or legal agreement.
  3. Someone searching for that document lands on the hacked page and downloads what looks like the file they wanted.
  4. The download is actually malware. It gives the criminals a way into that person's company network, which often ends in a ransomware attack, where files are locked until a payment is made.

According to the FBI, as of October 2026 it had identified more than 1,000 infected WordPress sites tied to this operation, and the scheme led to more than $28 million in ransomware payments. Search Engine Roundtable covered the alert on November 17.

Who is at risk

There are two ways this can touch your business.

Your website could be the one that gets hacked

The websites in this scheme were not run by criminals. They belonged to real organizations that had no idea their sites were being used. The criminals often hide their changes from the site owner, so everything can look normal when you visit your own homepage. The FBI's alert lists several other criminal groups doing similar things to WordPress sites, which shows how common this has become.

Your team could be the one that gets tricked

The FBI says this group has focused mainly on people who work in law, such as attorneys, in-house counsel and legal staff, because they often search for specific contract wording. But the same trick works on anyone who searches for a template, form or free download. If an employee opens one of these files on a work computer, the whole business can be affected.

Why this matters for your online reputation

Your website is the main place customers, Google and AI assistants like ChatGPT go to learn about your business. If it is quietly sending visitors to scams, that puts your customers at risk and can damage the trust you have worked to build, both with people and with the systems that decide which businesses to recommend. Keeping your site clean and secure is part of protecting your visibility, not a separate IT chore.

What to do this week

You do not need to be technical to take care of the basics. Share this list with whoever manages your website.

  1. Change your website passwords. Every person who logs in to your website, and your hosting account, should have a strong password that is not used anywhere else.
  2. Turn on two-step login. This means a code from a phone app or text is needed to sign in, so a stolen password alone is not enough.
  3. Remove old users. Delete logins for former employees, past agencies and anyone who no longer needs access. Look for any users you do not recognize.
  4. Update everything. Keep WordPress, your theme and every plugin up to date, and delete plugins you no longer use.
  5. Check what Google shows for your site. Search for site:yourdomain.com (using your own web address) and look for pages you did not create, especially ones about topics that have nothing to do with your business.
  6. Talk to your team about downloads. Ask staff to get templates and forms from sources they know and trust, and to tell someone right away if a download behaves strangely.

If you think you have been hacked

Do not try to quietly fix it and move on. The FBI recommends taking the site offline or limiting access, changing every related password from a different, clean computer, and finding out how the criminals got in before restoring a backup. Otherwise they can simply come back. The FBI also asks businesses to report suspected attacks through its Internet Crime Complaint Center.
